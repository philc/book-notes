# The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up - Marie Kondo

## Philosophy of tidying (chap 1)

* "A messy environment reflects a messy mind."
* Transforming the space into a tidy one changes your mindset and the state of the space going
  forward.
* Do it in one fell swoop.
* There are two parts to tidying: discarding and storing.
* Storage is not the problem. Everyone focuses on putting away things that you don't need. The room
  is still full of clutter. Eventually the storage containers will become full. Instead, begin with
  discarding.
* Tidy by category of thing -- clothes, books -- not by location. Otherwise, you will repeat a lot of
  the same work, because you store the same category of thing in multiple locations. You won't be
  able to see the entirety of your inventory if you do it by location.
* This book is about "special event tidying", not the mundane, essential daily activity of putting
  back the things that have been used.

## Finish discarding first (chap 2)

* Discarding must happen first. Do the full discarding process before spending any energy on where
  to keep things. It's very inefficient to intermingle the two. Storage efforts done (before
  discarding) can become inappropriate, depending on how much is discarded.
* How to decide what to discard:
  * Take each item in your hand, and ask, does this spark joy?
  * Don't visually sweep through a closet and make the judgement. Hold each thing, each outfit, and
    make the decision. Your body will react to the object.
  * (It's possible that "joy" takes the form of "even if I never use this thing, it helps me feel
    prepared for unexpected events.")
* Do not filter items which are not in use, or haven't been used in a year, or some criteria like
  that. This will only lead to unhappiness. It's a negative lens. Instead, seek to identify what you
  cherish, and keep those things.
* Imagine if your space was only filled with the things which give you joy. How great would that be?
* Tidy by category
  * To tidy by category, get it all out of the drawers and closets and gather it in one place that
    will allow you to make global optimization decisions. Things out of sight are dormant.
  * Don't start with mementos; it's the hardest category.
* Evaluation criteria to use for each object:
  * Functional value
  * Emotional value
  * Informational value
  * Rarity
* Tackle categories in this order of difficulty: clothes, books, papers, misc, mementos.
* Don't let anyone else in the family see what you've decided to throw out. It's emotionally hard
  for them to see things you've decided to part with.
* Avoid music if you can. You want sharp power of discernment. Music -- especially with lyrics --
  blunts that discernment. Instead, let the power of the room's atmosphere motivate you.

## Tidying up by category (chap 3)

* Clothes
  * Not all clothes come to you to be worn until they're threadbare. Some clothing items had the
    effect of teaching you which types of articles suit you or don't. Some were fun to buy, but not
    to wear. Their value has come and gone. When deciding to toss, it can be helpful to acknowledge
    the value with gratitude, but then say goodbye.
  * Feel free to personify your things. Let them go and live their lives. If they have been in a
    drawer for years and have been forgotten about, they don't like that sad lifestyle.
  * Get every piece of clothing out on the floor so you see how much you have, to better identify
    redundancy.
  * The average wardrobe contains 168 items.
  * Start with off seasonal clothes, then loungewear.
  * Don't downgrade everything that's now subpar to loungewear. What you wear around the house does
    affect your self image. Don't do sweats.
  * The problem with storing off-season clothes
    * Often you'll forget to take them out on time, and you'll start buying things which are
      similar. It's best if everything that you have is fully in view throughout the year, so you
      can prune your collection and add to it whenever you need to.
    * Off-seasonal clothes is an outdated notion: we use pants and T-shirts all year round.
  * Plastic bins with lids is the trickiest type of storage container to use, because often you
    stack things on top of this container and can't see what's inside. So it's much harder to get
    access to those things and keep them top of mind that you have still have them.
  * Don't try to categorize strictly by season, or "work vs. leisure." Those categories are too
    vague. It's better to arrange all of your clothes together, and then arrange items of similar
    weight and warmth together near each other.
* Books
  * The first step is to take them off the shelf and put them on the floor. You can't tell if a book
    gives you a thrill of joy if you're just looking at it on the shelf. Hold it, look at its cover,
    touch its pages. You will then be able to judge whether it still gives you joy, or whether you
    can move on from having it in your life.
  * Imagine if you had a bookshelf full of only the books that you love and give you joy. Wouldn't
    that be a great life? That's the goal.
  * Keep only those books that will move you when you see them on your shelves, even if you never
    plan to crack it open again.
  * What about unread books?
    * People accumulate many unread books because it's easy to order books online.
    * Timing is everything. The best time to read a book is when you first encounter it. If you keep
      your collection small, you can read books as they come in, when they're most relevant.
    * She recommends giving away old unread books. If the need to read it comes back around, order
      it again.
* Papers
  * Generally, throw every paper out.
  * You can scan anything you think you'll need a reference to, for e.g. taxes.
  * If it needs an action, take it.
  * Keep only a handful of files in a vertical inbox which should always be shallow. Get it done and
    get rid of it as soon as you can.
  * For sentimental papers like diplomas, love letters, keep those in a box somewhere.
* Gifts
  * Some find it hard to throw away unused gifts. E.g. a souvenir someone brought them that lives in
    a drawer unopened. It's valuable because somebody took their precious time to give that gift to
    you, so you feel bad just throwing it away. But note that gifts are meant to be received. The
    joy comes in transferring the thing from one person to another and the knowledge that they spent
    their time selecting it for you. If it's not useful, it doesn't mean you need to keep it. Nobody
    would want you to store their gift, never use it, and keep it in a drawer out of a sense of
    obligation. Thank it for the value it's given you, enjoy the memory of having received it, and
    then let it move on with its life.
* Mementos
  * Keep the ones that give you joy, and not because your kid wants you to keep their report card.

## Storing things (chap 4)

* Pick a designated spot for everything you own. If you have items in your house without a home,
  then the chances that they'll get lost or accumulate into piles of clutter.
* If you have a shelf with nothing on it and you put something on it that doesn't have a home, that
  shelf becomes a dumping ground for other random things.
* It's easy to maintain a house in order if you come home from work and can put down all of your
  things quickly and without thought.
* The reason she recommends discarding before optimizing the storage is because it's very easy to
  store things when you have very few things. People start with storage because it's a fun, rational
  problem to solve where you try to pack as many things you own into a given space as possible. But
  it's not productive. You shouldn't assume that everything you own is fixed, and take pride in all
  the junk that you own. Instead, we should be maximizing the amount of joy we get out of the items
  in our homes. Increase the "average joy per item" across our belongings.
* Keep storage very simple. Fancy storage mechanisms mask how much clutter one really has.
* For families, it is helpful to keep each person's stuff all in one room. If each person has
  designated places for their stuff in multiple places in the house, it's easy for this to result in
  clutter. Also, that person doesn't have a sense of ownership over their space. If a child is given
  their own room and all of their stuff is in that room, they will start to feel ownership of the
  space and have a desire to keep it tidy. The child will take interest in their clothes, start
  dressing themselves, and organize their toys so that they're not a big mess.
* If you treat your belongings as if they're alive, then you can express gratitude to them when you
  hang them up after a day's use. This will make you more appreciative of the things you have and
  increase the joy you derive from them. For instance, when you're done with your coat, thank it for
  keeping you warm all day. Do this as often as you can remember to.
* Clutter is caused by failure to return things to where they belong. So, storage should prioritize
  reducing the effort required to put things away, not the effort required to take them out.

## Tidying transforms lives (chap 5)

* Tidying is the process by which one selects all of the things they've accumulated in their life to
  reflect what they really enjoy and value. When somebody walks through a home that's well tidied,
  they will immediately get a sense of what that person is about and what they like. If their shelf
  is pruned to only include the books they love, it will become clear what field of study they're
  really drawn to, not just the ones they encountered as a student and professionally.
* The path that each thing took to arrive to you is special. That path makes a meaningful
  relationship between you and even a mass-market item. It's precious, and this helps build
  gratitude for and connection to the thing, even if you ultimately dispose of it.
* Human beings can only take care of and cherish so many things at a given time. Choose wisely.
* Being surrounded by things that bring you joy will make you happier.
* The reason the author spent so much of her life on tidying is because she gets an immense amount
  of satisfaction and exhilaration from sending possessions on their way, to be the most useful
  versions of themselves. She loves the idea of the universe clicking into place when an item finds
  its true owner and gives them joy.
