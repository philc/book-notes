Book Notes

Meditations for Mortals - Oliver Burkeman

Gems

  • "When you do what you feel like doing, you get to use your desires as fuel for action, rather than constantly diverting energy and attention to overcoming them."

Week One: Being Finite

  • On making sure you actually get to do what you want in life, rather than trying to create space to do so by developing systems: "Develop a willingness to just do something, here and now, as a one-off, regardless of whether it's part of any system or habit or routine."
  • The kayak and superyacht analogy of life
    • The kayak: you're hurtling down the river of life, not really in control. Making a perfect, far-sighted decision isn't realistic. Instead, you try to find something you can do, and you take that path.
    • You're "borne along on the river of time towards your inevitable yet unpredictable death. It's a thrilling situation, but also an intensely vulnerable one: you're at the mercy of the current, and all you can really do is to stay alert, steering as best you can, reacting as wisely and gracefully as possible to whatever arises from moment to moment."
    • Superyacht: you're hoping to smoothly sail through life and chart a well-planned course. You hesitate to move forward because you do not yet have the calm and far-sightedness of a superyacht.
  • "You are free to do whatever you like. You need only face the consequences." - Sheldon B. Kopp
    • In situations where there are only tough choices, it does no good to be frozen. Pick one with the comfort that you know you will be able to shoulder the consequences.
  • "Productivity debt": over-achievers start each day feeling in productivity debt. They must pay this debt to justify their existence and prove their worth. It's not a satisfying way to live.
  • A "Done" list vs a TODO list: "what makes a done list so motivating and encouraging is that it implicitly invites you to compare your output to the hypothetical situation in which you stayed in bed and did nothing at all."
  • Treat one's backlog as a river.
    • "Moving more quickly through an infinite incoming supply of something never gets you to the end of it."
    • This applies even to high-quality, relevant, educational content like books.
  • Spending attention on current events
    • The attention economy: all media, even noble outlets, are incentivized to exaggerate the importance of every event and story.
    • It's exhausting, and more so because the world is more connected than it's ever been. One can hear about every outrage that exists.
    • "In an age of attention scarcity, the greatest act of good citizenship may be learning to withdraw your attention from everything except the battles you've chosen to fight." Rather than getting upset about everything that's happening, most of which you can't affect.

Week Two: Taking Action

  • "You'd assume that seeing a project through to the end might leave you feeling depleted… yet the truth is that completion replenishes energy, rather than using it up."
  • The "life task"
    • "It's what's being asked of you, with your particular skills, resources and personality traits, in the place where you actually find yourself."
    • What does life want from me? "The question can be a startlingly powerful one, particularly when you find yourself torn between options."
    • It's an endeavor that enlarges you.
  • If you avoid problems because you're anxious about them, they are more likely to turn into serious problems. And along the way, they create a background hum of dread. Instead, run at the problems. Doing so disarms them.
  • Visualize yourself taking some action on that problem you've been avoiding. We're seeking some way to "'go there', psychologically speaking: to begin to accept, on an emotional level, that the situation in question is already part of your reality."
  • He argues that one can only do intense knowledge work for about three hours per day. There are many historical anecdotes suggesting this. So, do that work during one's peak, before lunch, and protect the time.

Week Three: Letting Go

  • Try to reframe a hard task: "what if this were easy?"
    • "Instead of asking how to summon the energy or motivation or self-discipline to do something that matters to you, it's often more helpful to ask: What if this might be a lot easier than I'd been assuming?"
    • And imagine: how, specifically, might it be easy?
  • Be careful not to equate effort with value.
    • Caring about a project does not mean it needs to be "complex or effortful" to be valuable.
  • "When you do what you feel like doing, you get to use your desires as fuel for action, rather than constantly diverting energy and attention to overcoming them."
    • We seek distraction like doom scrolling when we've decided that we can't do what we want.
  • Why does it matter to you if someone else is angry or impatiently waiting for your work? Don't let unnecessarily negative energy transfer to you.
  • When events are outside of our control, and we can't guarantee our plans will succeed: this is what makes life interesting.
  • "Almost everything that happens, according to an adage of uncertain origin, is either a good time or a good story. Either things go right, or they go wrong."
  • Use quantity as a goal instead of quality, to defeat the dread and inertia of trying to do something "high quality."
    • "'Here's the magic trick: if you can't come up with ten ideas, come up with twenty ideas.' Quantity overpowers perfectionism."
  • On interruptions
    • "The truth is of course that what one calls the interruptions are precisely one's real life — the life God is sending one day by day." - C.S. Lewis
    • "What looks like an interruption might well prove a welcome development."
    • "Once your focus has already been diverted — once the child has burst into the room… don't fight the fact. Deal with your new reality instead."
    • Don't be afraid to tell someone that you don't have time right now.

Week Four: Showing Up

  • "To treat life as a pilgrimage to a future and better existence is to disown its present value." - W. Somerset Maugham
  • It's natural to think that even if your life has some tranquility, more is to be desired, and the place you want to be is "somewhere else."
  • Rather than "clearing the decks", do the thing you want to be doing, with the first part of your day.
  • Decide who you want to be. Then act from that identity immediately.
  • Treating one's TODO list as a menu: "it's surprising how many things do become more appetizing once you're encountering them not as chores you have to plow through, but as options you get to pick."
  • Hosting friends only when your home feels perfectly clean will dissuade you from doing so, given it's a lot of work to make a presentable facade.
    • "A perfectly kept house is the sign of a misspent life." - Mary Randolph Carter
  • You can't hoard life. You have to let moments pass.
  • It's not realistic to find satisfying answers to why our life has become the way it is. It can be a form of analysis paralysis. Some people spend their whole lives trying to unpack their childhoods in therapy.
  • One can see their own insignificance by zooming out to the world stage, and particularly the great expanse of history. It can be relieving: reality does not need our supervision or effort to function and carry on.
  • Quietly and locally helping people is filled with meaning and dignity, even if it's not "at scale."
